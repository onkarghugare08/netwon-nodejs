A farm boy who rewrote physics

Born premature, raised by his grandmother, and a mediocre student at first, Newton became the most influential scientist of his age.

Newton grew up on a farm in Lincolnshire and went on to Trinity College, Cambridge. In 1665 the Great Plague closed the university, and he spent about two years back home, thinking.

In that stretch he worked out early versions of calculus, ran experiments with prisms, and began the line of thought that led to universal gravitation. Historians call it his annus mirabilis, a year of wonders.

His 1687 book, the Principia, gave one mathematical framework for falling objects, tides, planets and comets. It held up as the foundation of physics for more than two centuries.

Three laws of motion

The rules that explain how everything from a thrown ball to a rocket moves.

1

Inertia

An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a force acts on it.

A hockey puck glides until friction stops it.
2

Force equals mass times acceleration

The harder you push something, the more it accelerates. The heavier it is, the less.

F = ma
3

Action and reaction

When one body pushes on another, the second pushes back on the first with equal force in the opposite direction.

A rocket moves forward by throwing gas backward.

A life in seven moments

  1. 1643

    Born at Woolsthorpe Manor

    Arrives early in Lincolnshire, so small his mother said he could fit in a quart mug.

  2. 1661

    Enters Trinity College

    Studies at Cambridge and starts reading the new science on his own.

  3. 1665

    The plague years

    Back home, he develops calculus, studies light, and thinks hard about gravity.

  4. 1687

    Publishes the Principia

    Sets out the laws of motion and universal gravitation. His fame is secured.

  5. 1699

    Master of the Mint

    Moves to London and reforms the nation’s currency.

  6. 1704

    Opticks appears

    His experiments on light and colour reach a wide audience.

  7. 1727

    Dies in London

    Buried in Westminster Abbey, an honour then rare for a scientist.
